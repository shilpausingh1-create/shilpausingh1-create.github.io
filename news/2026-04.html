April 2026 in AI: An AI too risky to release

Anthropic held back its hacking-capable Mythos model, OpenAI launched GPT-5.5, Meta cut 8,000 jobs, and AI company values neared $1 trillion.

EveryoneJobs & CareersFinanceBusinessGovernment & LawDevelopers

April was the month AI safety stopped being theory, as Anthropic built a model so good at finding security holes that it refused to release it widely. Meanwhile, the race kept speeding up: a new OpenAI model, big job cuts at Meta, and a rewritten deal between OpenAI and Microsoft. By the end of the month, Anthropic was in talks to become worth more than OpenAI.

1. Anthropic holds back powerful hacking-capable Mythos model

What happened: Anthropic announced Claude Mythos Preview, a model that is extremely good at finding weaknesses in software. Instead of releasing it to everyone, it gave access only to partners in a program called Project Glasswing, including Apple, Google, Microsoft, Nvidia and Amazon Web Services, plus more than 40 other companies like CrowdStrike and Palo Alto Networks. In one case it found a 27-year-old bug in OpenBSD, a system known for security. TechCrunch later reported Mythos had found thousands of serious bugs.

Why it matters: The same skill that helps defenders fix holes could help criminals break in. Holding it back gives the "good guys" a head start to patch their systems before attackers get similar tools. Anthropic also committed up to $100 million in free usage for this defensive work, and CEO Dario Amodei said getting it right could make the internet "fundamentally more secure."

Who it affects: Banks, hospitals, utilities, IT and security teams, governments.

Source: CNBC (second report: TechCrunch)

2. OpenAI launches GPT-5.5, its smartest model yet

What happened: OpenAI released GPT-5.5, less than two months after GPT-5.4. It is better at coding, using computers, research, and making documents and spreadsheets. OpenAI rated it "High" for cybersecurity risk, one step below its "Critical" level. OpenAI's president said what's special is "how much more it can do with less guidance."

Why it matters: New top models now arrive every few weeks. Each one can handle messier, less defined tasks on its own. That speed makes it hard for workers, schools and lawmakers to keep up, and OpenAI said it tested GPT-5.5 with outside experts for cyber and biology risks before release.

Who it affects: Office workers, developers, students, IT security teams.

Source: CNBC

3. Meta to cut 10% of staff for AI

What happened: Meta said it would lay off about 8,000 workers, 10% of its staff, starting May 20, and scrap plans to fill 6,000 open roles. It also said it would rely on AI instead of outside contractors for content moderation. Days later, Meta raised its 2026 spending plan to $125 billion to $145 billion, much of it for AI, according to the AP. Microsoft offered voluntary buyouts to some U.S. staff the same week.

Why it matters: Big tech is moving money from people to computers. Meta also revealed it is recording employee keystrokes and mouse clicks to train AI agents. For workers, it is a clear sign that AI is already reshaping who gets hired, and Meta's workforce has fallen from 86,482 in late 2022 to 78,865 at the end of 2025.

Who it affects: Tech workers, content moderators, contractors, job seekers, HR teams.

Source: CNBC (spending figure: AP via NBC Bay Area)

4. OpenAI and Microsoft rewrite their partnership

What happened: OpenAI and Microsoft changed their deal. OpenAI can now sell all its products on any cloud, including Amazon and Google. Its revenue-share payments to Microsoft, reportedly 20%, continue through 2030 but now have a total cap. Microsoft keeps a license to OpenAI's technology through 2032, but it is no longer exclusive, and Microsoft stops paying OpenAI a share when customers use OpenAI models through Microsoft's cloud.

Why it matters: For years, Microsoft was OpenAI's main gate to business customers. Now OpenAI can reach companies wherever they already work. For businesses, it means more choice in where and how they buy AI, and Amazon said it would soon offer OpenAI models to its own cloud customers.

Who it affects: Business IT buyers, cloud companies like Amazon and Google, and investors in Microsoft and Amazon.

Source: CNBC

5. Anthropic in talks at $900 billion valuation

What happened: Anthropic was in talks to raise money at a $900 billion value, which would top OpenAI's $852 billion. In February it was worth $380 billion. Anthropic said its yearly revenue pace reached $30 billion, up from about $10 billion in revenue last year. Amazon agreed to invest up to $25 billion and Google up to $40 billion.

Why it matters: Anthropic was long seen as the underdog to OpenAI. Its fast growth, mostly from businesses and its coding tool Claude Code, shows where the money in AI is right now: work tools. A source said the money is needed partly to buy computing power to run Mythos, and Anthropic has also signed deals for huge amounts of computing from Amazon, Google and Broadcom.

Who it affects: Investors, business leaders, developers and companies that rely on Claude.

Source: CNBC

What to watch next

  • Whether Anthropic closes its funding round and becomes the most valuable AI startup, passing OpenAI for the first time.
  • How and when Anthropic makes Mythos-class models more widely available, since it said the goal is to learn how to release them safely at scale.
  • Meta's layoffs, which begin May 20, and whether other tech giants follow with their own cuts or buyouts.
  • AWS offering OpenAI models through its Bedrock service, which Amazon said would come within weeks.
