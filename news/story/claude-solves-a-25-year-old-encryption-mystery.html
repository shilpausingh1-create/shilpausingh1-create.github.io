Claude solves a 25-year-old encryption mystery

By the ITG Now newsroom · News · 16 Oct 2026, 22:32 IST · Updated 19 Oct 2026

Share

Corrected 19 Oct: Anthropic says a backdoor is 'much less plausible', not ruled out. B-163 and some base points are still unexplained. All corrections

Anthropic says its AI, Claude, has worked out where the secret numbers behind some of the world's most-used encryption curves came from. The answer is plain: a 1990s random number tool and the computer clock, not a hidden trick. Anthropic says no action is needed from users.

What happened

In a research post, Anthropic explains the origin of the "seeds" used for the NIST elliptic curves. These curves are part of ECDSA, a widely used way of signing data digitally. NIST adopted ECDSA in 2000. The curves came with published seed numbers, but nobody explained where the seeds came from. Some people feared the NSA, which supplied them, had hidden a weakness.

The post says an Anthropic employee asked an internal research model whether it could work out how the seeds were made, without pointing at the clue. Anthropic says Claude did so in under half an hour. The seeds were not the output of a hash, as an older account had claimed. They came from lrand48, a random number generator in the C programming language, started with a Unix timestamp, which is just the clock time.

Anthropic says the timestamps for the prime-field curves fall between May 1997 and April 1998. For the binary-field curves, the dates land in April 1998 if the timestamps are doubled. Anthropic says the program may have halved the clock.

Key facts

  • NIST adopted ECDSA in 2000 in FIPS 186-2, per Anthropic.
  • Nine NIST curve seeds were explained: five prime-field (P-192, P-224, P-256, P-384 and P-521) and four binary-field (B-233, B-283, B-409 and B-571).
  • The seed for one curve, B-163, is still unexplained.
  • Claude noticed that the top bit of every 32-bit word was zero in four seeds. Anthropic puts the odds of that by chance at about 1 in 5,810.
  • Anthropic says this is not a cryptographic break or a new attack on ECDSA.
  • A bounty of $36,864 will go to the Internet Security Research Group, according to Anthropic.

Background

For years, a well-known cryptographer ran a public bounty, posted in October 2023, for finding the lost starting inputs for the five prime-curve seeds. It paid triple, $36,864, if the winner gave it to a US charity. That post said the seeds were reportedly produced by an NSA employee in 1997 by hashing English sentences whose wording was lost. It also said that the employee died in early 2023, so the story could not be checked.

Anthropic says that older clue turned out to be wrong, and the seeds were not the result of any hash at all.

What it means for you

Your online accounts are not suddenly more or less safe. Techmeme links the news as Anthropic saying Claude traced NIST's ECDSA curve seeds to a pseudorandom number generator. Anthropic says no action is needed. But a long-running worry has a simple, checkable answer.

Anthropic says a pseudorandom generator started from the clock makes it more confident the curves were chosen without backdoors or intentional weaknesses. Our take: that is not the same as proof there was never a backdoor. The authors themselves note some limits. Some usable candidates were skipped for reasons nobody knows, and the base points of several curves are still unexplained.

Our take: the more interesting point is the method. Anthropic says its AI wrote a scanner that slid along each published seed one bit at a time, and found a pattern that had gone unexplained for years.

What happens next

The authors released their reproduction code under a CC0 license, so anyone can check the work. Our take: watch for other cryptographers to confirm or challenge the findings.

FAQ

Does this mean my passwords or payments are unsafe?

No. Anthropic says this is not a break of ECDSA and no action is needed from users.

Was there an NSA backdoor?

Anthropic says the clock-based origin makes a hidden backdoor much less likely. It does not say it can be ruled out, and some questions remain.

Where did the seeds come from?

Per Anthropic, they came from the lrand48 random generator started with a timestamp from 1997 or 1998.
