March 2026 in AI: OpenAI goes big, then gets serious

OpenAI raised a record $122 billion, shut down Sora, and launched GPT-5.4, while a judge sided with Anthropic and Nvidia forecast $1 trillion in orders.

EveryoneFinanceJobs & CareersBusinessCreatorsGovernment & LawDevelopers

March was OpenAI's month: it raised the most money any private company ever has, then cut a popular app to focus on work tools. Meanwhile, a court pushed back on the Pentagon, and Nvidia made it clear that the AI building boom is far from over. Here is what happened and why it matters beyond Silicon Valley.

1. OpenAI raises record $122 billion

What happened: OpenAI closed a $122 billion funding round, valuing it at $852 billion. Amazon agreed to invest up to $50 billion, Nvidia $30 billion and SoftBank $30 billion. For the first time, OpenAI also let regular investors in through banks, raising $3 billion from individuals. It said it now makes $2 billion in revenue a month, up from $13.1 billion for all of last year, but it is still losing money.

Why it matters: This is the largest private funding round on record. That much money comes with pressure: OpenAI now has to prove it is worth nearly a trillion dollars, especially as it eyes a stock market listing. It has already started cutting costs and dropping side projects, which leads to the next story.

Who it affects: Investors, everyday savers watching AI stocks, business leaders.

Source: CNBC

2. OpenAI shuts down its Sora video app

What happened: OpenAI closed Sora, its TikTok-style app for AI-made videos, about six months after launch. Sora had hit one million downloads in under five days, but the excitement faded. OpenAI is cutting costs and focusing on work tools, and it dropped its Instant Checkout shopping feature the same day. A planned $1 billion Disney deal to let users make videos with Disney characters never closed.

Why it matters: AI video is very expensive to make, and a viral launch does not equal a lasting business. OpenAI's apps chief said the company is "orienting aggressively" toward productivity, where Anthropic has been winning. For creators, it is a reminder that AI tools can disappear fast, so always keep your own copies of anything you make on someone else's platform.

Who it affects: Video creators, social media users, Hollywood studios, marketers.

Source: CNBC

3. OpenAI releases GPT-5.4 that can use computers

What happened: OpenAI released GPT-5.4, which it called its most capable model "for professional work." It set records on tests where AI operates a computer and websites by itself. It scored a record 83% on OpenAI's GDPval test, which compares AI work with professionals across real job tasks. OpenAI said it makes 33% fewer errors in individual claims than GPT-5.2, and its answers are 18% less likely to contain any error at all.

Why it matters: An AI that can click, type and use apps like a person can do whole tasks, not just answer questions. Testers said it excels at slide decks, financial models and legal analysis. That puts it squarely in the territory of many office jobs, from analysts to paralegals, so it is worth learning how these tools work in your field.

Who it affects: Office workers, lawyers, finance teams, consultants, developers.

Source: TechCrunch

4. Judge blocks Pentagon's punishment of Anthropic

What happened: After the Pentagon labeled Anthropic a "supply chain risk" and the government ordered agencies to stop using Claude, a federal judge in California blocked those moves. Judge Rita Lin wrote that nothing supports "the Orwellian notion" that a U.S. company can be branded an adversary for disagreeing with the government. She said the Pentagon's own records showed the label came from Anthropic's "hostile manner through the press."

Why it matters: The ruling said punishing a company for speaking out is likely illegal under the First Amendment (free speech). It protects Anthropic's contracts for now, which the company said were worth hundreds of millions of dollars. But the Pentagon's technology chief called the ruling a "disgrace" and signaled an appeal, and a second case was still pending in Washington, D.C.

Who it affects: Government contractors, lawyers, AI companies, anyone who cares about free speech.

Source: CNN

5. Nvidia predicts $1 trillion in AI chip orders

What happened: At its yearly GTC conference, Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang said he expects orders for its Blackwell and Vera Rubin chips to reach $1 trillion through 2027. A year earlier the forecast was $500 billion. Nvidia is now the world's most valuable public company, worth about $4.5 trillion. Huang also announced a deal for Uber to run self-driving cars on Nvidia software in 28 cities by 2028.

Why it matters: Nvidia's chips are the engine of the AI boom, so its forecast is a signal for the whole economy. Doubling the forecast means companies keep spending huge sums on AI, and Huang said customers would earn more if they could just get more computing capacity. The self-driving news shows AI moving into transport too, with Nissan, BYD, Hyundai and others building self-driving cars on Nvidia's platform.

Who it affects: Investors, retirement savers, drivers, carmakers, energy companies.

Source: CNBC

What to watch next

  • The Pentagon's appeal of Judge Lin's ruling, and the separate case in Washington, D.C.
  • OpenAI's push toward a possible stock market listing, and whether it can justify its $852 billion price tag while still losing money.
  • Nvidia's Vera Rubin system, due to ship later this year, which Nvidia says will deliver 10 times more performance per watt of electricity.
  • Uber's Nvidia-powered robotaxis, starting in Los Angeles and San Francisco next year.
