January 2026 in AI: AI moves into phones, health, ads

Apple bet Siri on Google, Amazon cut 16,000 office jobs, ChatGPT added health records and ads, and two countries blocked Grok.

EveryoneJobs & CareersHealthcareMarketingMediaGovernment & LawBusiness

January set the tone for the whole year, as AI stopped being a side feature and started moving into the things we use every day: our phones, our health questions, and even the ads we see. It also showed the costs, from lost office jobs to fake images that hurt real people. Here are the five stories that mattered most, and what they mean for people outside the tech world.

1. Apple picks Google's Gemini to power new Siri

What happened: Apple signed a multi-year deal to build its next AI models on Google's Gemini and Google's cloud technology. Apple said that "after careful evaluation" Google offered "the most capable foundation" for its own AI. The models will still run on Apple devices and on Apple's private cloud, and Apple did not share the price. Bloomberg had earlier reported Apple planned to pay about $1 billion a year for Google's AI.

Why it matters: Apple is famous for building everything itself. Choosing a rival's AI shows how hard it is to catch up in this race, even for one of the richest companies on Earth, and it is a big win for Google in its comeback against OpenAI. For iPhone owners, it means a smarter Siri, which Apple had already delayed from 2025 to 2026.

Who it affects: iPhone and Mac users, app makers, investors in Apple and Google.

Source: CNBC

2. Amazon cuts 16,000 office jobs amid AI push

What happened: Amazon said it would cut about 16,000 corporate jobs, its second big round in three months after 14,000 cuts in October. Together that is about 30,000 jobs, or roughly 10% of its 350,000 office and tech workers. Amazon framed the cuts as "removing bureaucracy," while also planning to spend about $125 billion in 2026, much of it on AI and data centers (giant buildings full of computers). Amazon also closed its Fresh and Go grocery chains that same week to free up money.

Why it matters: CEO Andy Jassy said last year that AI would likely shrink Amazon's office headcount, adding "we will need fewer people doing some of the jobs that are being done today." When one of the world's biggest employers says this out loud, other companies tend to follow, so office workers everywhere should pay attention to which tasks AI is taking over in their own field.

Who it affects: Office workers, managers, HR teams, job seekers, investors.

Source: CNBC

3. Countries block Grok over fake sexual images

What happened: Indonesia and Malaysia blocked Elon Musk's AI chatbot Grok after it was used to make sexual "deepfakes" (fake but realistic images) of real women, and abuse images involving children. Malaysia's regulator said X's replies relied too much on users reporting problems and did not fix how the tool was designed. A UK child safety group said it found criminal images of children aged 11 to 13 that appear to have been made with Grok. The EU, the UK, Brazil and India also called for investigations into the tool.

Why it matters: This was one of the first times whole countries cut off a major AI tool over harm to real people. It shows governments are willing to act fast when AI image tools are easy to misuse, and Indonesia's minister called fake sexual images a form of "digital-based violence." It also raised pressure on app stores and platforms to police AI features.

Who it affects: Parents, schools, social media users, regulators, platform safety teams.

Source: CNBC

4. ChatGPT Health lets users link medical records

What happened: OpenAI launched ChatGPT Health, a separate space for health conversations. It said more than 230 million people ask ChatGPT health and wellness questions every week. Users can connect wellness apps and records such as Apple Health, Function and MyFitnessPal, and OpenAI says it will not use Health chats to train its models. If you start a health chat in the normal ChatGPT window, it will nudge you to move it into the Health space, so your health details stay separate from your other chats.

Why it matters: Millions of people already ask a chatbot before they ask a doctor. A dedicated health space could help with cost, access and overbooked doctors, as OpenAI argues. But chatbots can still make things up, and OpenAI's own terms say it is "not intended for use in the diagnosis or treatment" of health conditions.

Who it affects: Patients, doctors, nurses, health insurers, wellness app makers.

Source: TechCrunch

5. Ads are coming to ChatGPT

What happened: OpenAI said it would start testing ads for adult free users in the U.S. It also launched its low-cost Go plan in the U.S., which will also show ads. Paid Plus, Pro and Enterprise plans stay ad-free. Ads will sit at the bottom of answers, be clearly labeled, and will not appear near topics like politics, health or mental health.

Why it matters: AI is very expensive to run, and ads are how Google and Meta got rich. OpenAI says ads will not change ChatGPT's answers and it will "never" sell user data. For brands, a brand-new ad channel is opening right where people go to ask for advice, compare products and plan purchases.

Who it affects: Marketers, advertisers, media companies, everyday ChatGPT users.

Source: CNBC

What to watch next

  • Whether Apple's Google-powered Siri actually ships in 2026, as Apple promised.
  • Whether Amazon makes more cuts. Its HR chief did not rule them out but said it is "not our plan" to cut every few months.
  • More investigations into Grok in the EU, UK, Brazil and India.
  • How ChatGPT ads perform, and whether OpenAI keeps its promises on labeling and privacy.
